How to Become a CNA in California: A Step-by-Step 2026 Guide

Written by Tynesha Zacarias, RN, Lead Instructor · Updated July 14, 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • To become a CNA in California you must complete a CDPH-approved training program, clear a Live Scan background check, and pass a two-part state competency exam.
  • California requires at least 160 hours of training, typically 60 classroom hours plus 100 supervised clinical hours.
  • Only training from a CDPH-approved program qualifies you for the state exam, so verify approval before you enroll.
  • The state exam has a written knowledge test and a hands-on skills evaluation, and you must pass both.
  • Most students finish in about 4 to 16 weeks, then appear on the California nurse assistant registry once certified.
  • CNAs renew every two years with 48 hours of continuing education, and most employers also require a current AHA BLS card.

Learning how to become a CNA in California is a common first question for people entering healthcare, and the good news is that the path is short and clear. Becoming a Certified Nursing Assistant is one of the fastest and most affordable ways to start a hands-on healthcare career in the state. In a matter of weeks, not years, you can earn a state certificate that lets you work in hospitals, skilled nursing facilities, hospice, home health, and more. It is also a proven stepping stone toward becoming an LVN or RN later on.

This guide walks you through every step of the process as it stands in 2026, written and reviewed by a Registered Nurse. We cover who is eligible, how to pick a state-approved program, the required training hours, the Live Scan background check, the two-part state competency exam, and how you finally land on the California nurse assistant registry. If you are in the Sacramento region, we also show how our CNA program in Orangevale fits into this path.

What a CNA Does and Why It Is a Smart First Step

A Certified Nursing Assistant provides direct, day-to-day care under the supervision of licensed nurses. That includes helping patients with bathing, dressing, eating, and moving safely, taking vital signs, and being the eyes and ears that alert the nursing team when something changes. It is demanding, deeply human work, and it is often where the best nurses first learn what real bedside care looks like.

The demand is steady. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, employers are expected to have roughly 211,800 openings for nursing assistants and orderlies each year on average over the coming decade, mostly to replace workers who move on or retire. You can review the current national numbers directly in the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook for Nursing Assistants. In California, wages tend to run higher than the national median because of the cost of living and strong demand, though your pay will vary by employer, setting, and experience.

Step 1: Confirm You Meet the Basic Eligibility Requirements

California keeps the entry requirements straightforward. To begin a nurse assistant training program and pursue certification, you generally need to:

  • Be at least 16 years old.
  • Provide a valid government-issued photo ID and a Social Security number.
  • Be able to read, write, and communicate in English well enough to complete training and the state exam.
  • Be physically able to perform patient-care tasks, which programs confirm with a health screening, and clear a criminal background check.

You do not need a high school diploma or any prior healthcare experience to start. What you do need is reliability, compassion, and a willingness to learn skills the right way, because they matter at the bedside. If you have questions about your specific situation, our team is happy to talk it through on the enrollment page before you commit.

Step 2: Choose a CDPH-Approved Training Program

This is the step where people most often go wrong, so read it carefully. In California, you can only sit for the state exam if you complete a training program that is approved by the California Department of Public Health (CDPH). A course that is not on the approved list will not qualify you, no matter how good it looks. Always confirm a school's approval before you pay anything. You can verify current program and certification rules at the official CDPH Nurse Assistant Training Program page.

When comparing schools, look at more than price. Ask about class size, how much real clinical time you get, pass rates, instructor experience, and whether the school helps you through the application and testing paperwork. A good program does not just teach to the test, it prepares you to be safe and confident on your first shift. Step 3: Complete the Required Training Hours

California requires more classroom and clinical time than the federal minimum. As of 2026, an approved program must include at least 160 total hours of training, typically broken into a minimum of 60 hours of classroom theory and 100 hours of supervised clinical practice with real patients. Because requirements can be updated, always confirm the current hour breakdown with your program and with CDPH.

Depending on whether you attend full time or part time, most students finish in roughly 4 to 16 weeks. The classroom portion covers infection control, safety, resident rights, communication, body mechanics, and basic nursing care. The clinical portion is where it becomes real: you practice those skills on actual patients under direct supervision until they become second nature. Do not rush this part. The habits you build in clinicals are the ones you will rely on when a patient is depending on you.

Step 4: Complete the Live Scan Background Check

Every California CNA candidate must clear a criminal background check through Live Scan fingerprinting before certification. In most cases you complete a Live Scan fingerprint request form (the BCIA 8016) and submit your initial application to CDPH before you start the patient-care portion of your training. Many programs, including ours, submit the application on your behalf and walk you through the fingerprinting appointment so nothing falls through the cracks.

If you have a prior conviction, it does not automatically disqualify you. CDPH reviews the nature and severity of the offense, how long ago it happened, your compliance with any court requirements, and your evidence of rehabilitation. If this applies to you, be honest and thorough on your application and provide documentation. When in doubt, contact CDPH directly before enrolling so you know where you stand.

Step 5: Pass the State Competency Exam

Once you finish an approved program, you take the state competency evaluation, administered in California through the National Nurse Aide Assessment Program (NNAAP). It has two parts, and you must pass both:

  • The written (knowledge) test: roughly 70 questions covering areas like infection control, safety, resident rights, communication, and basic care, with about two hours to complete it. An oral version is available for eligible candidates who need it.
  • The skills evaluation: you demonstrate a small set of randomly selected nurse assistant skills, usually five, in front of an evaluator within a set time. Every critical step of each skill must be performed correctly, so practice until the sequence is automatic.

Handwashing and measuring vital signs are common skills, but you will not know your exact five until test day, so you must be solid on all of them. This is exactly why real clinical practice and good instruction matter. Programs that give you plenty of hands-on repetition tend to see students pass the first time.

Step 6: Get on the California Nurse Assistant Registry

When you pass both parts of the exam and your background check clears, CDPH issues your certificate and adds you to the California nurse assistant registry. That registry listing is what employers check to confirm you are certified and in good standing, so your certificate is official once you appear there. At that point you can apply for CNA jobs and legally work as a Certified Nursing Assistant in the state.

Your certification is not permanent on its own. To stay active, California CNAs must renew every two years and complete 48 hours of approved continuing education during each renewal cycle, with a set minimum each year and a cap on how many hours may be taken online. When you are ready for that step, our CNA continuing education hours make renewal simple. Many new CNAs also add an AHA BLS CPR certification right away, since most employers require a current BLS card and AHA cards are generally valid for about two years.

Timeline, Cost, and Your Local Next Step

Here is the realistic picture. From your first day of class to your name appearing on the registry, most students in California move through the process in about 4 to 16 weeks, depending on schedule and how quickly exam and application steps line up. Budget for the training program itself plus separate costs for Live Scan fingerprinting and the state exam, which are paid to outside vendors and can change.
